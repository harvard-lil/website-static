## Panelist Introductions

**Molly Hardy:** Now, without further ado, I'm going to turn to the panelists, introducing them and then asking each to share their own professional contributions and work with the census and how we understand it. All four of our panelists are incredibly accomplished, so please pardon me for keeping introductions short so that we can proceed to the heart of the matter.

First, we'll hear from danah boyd, who is the Gary Gay Professor of Communication at Cornell University. Dr. boyd's research focuses on the intersections of technology and society, with an eye towards how structural inequalities shape and are shaped by technologies. How fitting, then, that her latest book, _Data Are Made, Not Found_, is a story of the civil servants who work tirelessly, and increasingly under duress, to produce the census.

Next, we'll hear from Cynthia Dwork, who is the Gordon McKay Professor of Computer Science here at Harvard at the John A. Paulson School of Engineering and Applied Sciences, and an affiliated faculty member here at the law school. Many of you know Cynthia for her pioneering work in differential privacy and its applications in protecting personal privacy in the census.

I encourage you to check out the editorial she published this summer in _The Boston Globe_, co-authored with our next panelist, Ruth Greenwood, who is an assistant clinical professor and director of the Election Law Clinic here at Harvard Law School. An expert in both election law and redistricting, Ruth previously co-directed Voting Rights and Redistricting at the Campaign Legal Center. She was lead counsel for voting rights at the Chicago Lawyers' Committee for Civil Rights Under Law and was a redistricting fellow with the Democratic National Committee's Voting Rights Institute.

And finally, a welcome home to Jayshree Sarathy, Assistant Professor of Computer Science at Northeastern University. Jayshree completed her PhD here at Harvard in Computer Science, where she was advised by Salil Vadhan and Jonathan Zittrain. And just to bring things full circle, in 2022, Jayshree and danah jointly published an article titled "[Differential Perspectives: Epistemic Disconnects Surrounding the US Census Bureau's Use of Differential Privacy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18648)." Her work focuses on the sociotechnical dynamics of data science, with a focus on privacy, trust, and data access, all areas very germane to our conversation today.

So let's please get started with danah — if you could just start by telling us about your engagements with the census.

**danah boyd:** Sure. Thank you all for coming out today. Indeed, this book came out last Thursday. I'm an ethnographer, which means that my work is to think about mapping cultural practice. This book is the culmination of 10 years of trying to understand what makes data legitimate, in the most high-stakes data we have. After all, if you think about it, it is a very weird thing that we have actually architected the US Constitution to anchor our representative democracy in the idea of collecting data and allocating political power accordingly.

And so for the last 230-plus years, we've embarked on this project of how to understand who our country is in the form of the population. This has had lots of crazy twists and turns. Margo Anderson wrote [a brilliant book about the social history of the US census](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300195422/the-american-census/), so if you want to understand its history, because it's crazy, everything you thought you understood about the US, you can actually read through the census. It's fun.

But my goal was to understand what it takes to actually do this in real time. So I embedded inside the US Census Bureau to really appreciate what they were doing. Part of it is the recognition that we don't often acknowledge the important work that civil servants do. They are the invisible workers. They are the people that just do the work on behalf of the American people, but they are constantly maligned.

I grew up in this country constantly being told that civil servants were stupid or malevolent, incompetent, that it could be done better by industry, that this is just the deep state. You've heard all of it. And civil servants unfortunately often even believe it, which is actually terrifying. But these people are so dedicated to a mission: let's make certain that we have the best count possible. And so I wanted to tell their story. This book is character-driven. It tells their story in different ways through the various ups and downs of the 2020 census. So if you want to know about citizenship, if you want to know about residence rules, if you want to know about differential privacy, it's here for you.

For the arguments in this book, I have three threads, and I want to highlight them to give you a sense. The first is that data are made, not found. That's the title. But the point is that it's not just, "Oh, the categories are socially constructed." It's a series of ongoing decisions, made with an eye to making the best data possible. To make that really concrete, I went back to 1880 — I was just too lazy to go further — and I looked from 1880 to 1950 at the age variable, how old are you, within census data. And it turns out that you get these spikes in years ending in zero and five, because humans round their age. They're not 72, they're 70.

And they don't just round about their own age. They round about their housemate's age. They round about their neighbor's age. They don't tell enumerators their actual age, and the enumerators are often too scared to ask them details. So they just look at you and are like, "You look like you're 50. That sounds good." And we move on. The Census Bureau receives this data, and if they published it as is, people would think they were nuts. So they go through all of these procedures, not just collecting the data but figuring out how to make it into democracy's data. That's the first big through line.

The next big through line is an argument about Jenga politics, which is going to be relevant today. Jenga is that wooden tower. Think about it as our administrative state, as people have been taking out pieces from it and adding pressure on top. People have been doing this for all sorts of different reasons, both Republicans and Democrats, for a long time. I tell lots of cases of where this occurs, where help is not helpful. Things that will also upend your idea of, "Oh, but participatory engagement, this is really helpful" — except when it's not.

Civil servants are running around trying to keep that wobbly tower from falling over, but they need help. And I'd like to remind everybody, somebody will get blamed when the tower falls, and people will celebrate because it's not their political party or whatever, but we all lose, and we all contributed to it. We need to understand how this Jenga tower is falling apart. If we care about democracy, and if we care about data, this becomes really important.

The final through line has to do with what makes data legitimate. You might have heard lots of stories about the 2020 census. You might be convinced that it was a terrible census. Here's the thing: the data for the 2020 census were better than all data collected in the 19th and 20th centuries by the censuses. They were maybe more problematic than what happened in 2000 and 2010. But when you interrogate that, it turns out the problem is not differential privacy. It is not radical malfeasance or political dynamics. It is that a pandemic is a problem. It's really hard to collect data during a pandemic.

> The legitimacy of data isn't rooted in some magical metric of data quality. It's rooted in the social fabric that we build, the trust infrastructure, the ways we understand the systems.

But what does it mean that we believe these data to be crap? It's really important to understand the consequences of our delegitimization of this. The legitimacy of data isn't rooted in some magical metric of data quality. It's rooted in the social fabric that we build, the trust infrastructure, the ways we understand the systems. I try to help you see which attacks on legitimacy are attacks on the social fabric. Because if we want to have nice things, we have to attend to the social fabric and not just think that it's going to get magically fixed through law or through policy. It is going to be a lot of relational work, and that is as true for our data as it is for our broader political polarization issues.

**Cynthia Dwork:** So why don't we start with what differential privacy is, because you may or may not know. Differential privacy is a mathematical guarantee. It says that the outcome of any statistical analysis is essentially equally likely whether any individual opts in or opts out of the dataset. So where does this likelihood come from?

The answer is we achieve differential privacy by the introduction of carefully calibrated noise. If the true answer for some enumeration is, say, 10,000, you might get an output of 10,002 or 9,996. And if the true enumeration is 10,001, you might also get those same numbers, just with slightly different probabilities. That's where the randomness comes in, and that's what "essentially equally likely" means. In the context of the census, this means that any collection of published statistics is essentially equally likely whether you chose to respond to the census or you decided not to. That's all.

The idea is that if outcomes happen with essentially the same probability whether you joined or not, they're really not leaking much information about you personally, because they wouldn't have leaked it if you hadn't joined. For any statistic or collection of statistics, there are zillions of potential differentially private algorithms, and the algorithms vary in their accuracy and noise trade-offs. Some might be optimized for one kind of data, some for a different kind. But the mathematical guarantee is always absolute.

So differential privacy does not mean the particular top-down algorithm that the Census Bureau used. It's just a mathematical definition of privacy, and different computational methods will give you different instantiations of it. I wanted to mention also why it's called "differential." Everybody receives the same mathematical guarantee, and the term "differential" actually comes from calculus, where it refers to a small change in a quantity.

In the early days, we had a bound on these relative probabilities, and the bound had some name, say epsilon, and we didn't know whether to call it epsilon privacy or epsilon non-privacy. Mike Schroeder suggested, "Why don't you call it differential privacy?" And that seemed to be exactly mathematically the right thing. But everybody gets the same privacy protection. I think I'll stop there.

**Ruth Greenwood:** I'm not good at math, so it's quite intimidating to do anything after Cynthia Dwork. But I do know how to work with incredible people. My name is Ruth; I run the clinic here. I'm from Australia originally, but my career in America has been litigating voting rights and democracy cases.

When we think about democracy, we may all have different conceptions of it, but it will probably include some notion of the people and some notion of decision-makers. And in order to know who the people are in a democracy, you need information, you need data. Here I have printed out the [Federal Register proposal](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/09/10/2026-18481/decennial-census-of-the-population-of-americans-proposed-residence-criteria-and-proposed-regulations) to change the census, just because it makes me mad. There were parts where I wrote "CD," as in "Cynthia Dwork needs to deal with this," and other parts where I had my own thoughts.

> Race is a social construct, but once you have constructed it, it has deep meaning in our society. To put your fingers in your ears and pretend it doesn't exist gets you nowhere.

It quotes people like Justice Thomas saying that race is a social construct. So what they're proposing to do is remove the race and ethnicity questions, among other things, as in prohibit them. As I like to say to my students, race is a social construct, but once you have constructed it, it has deep meaning in our society. To put your fingers in your ears and pretend it doesn't exist gets you nowhere. In fact, it moves you backwards.

It's the same thing when I talk about partisan gerrymandering. If you just look at a blank map of Massachusetts and draw some circles, without considering where there are Ds and Rs, you're not going to know what you get. You know what's better? Knowing what you're going to get, caring about the values that go into that, and making an informed decision. So it's very important to me that we have a sufficiently accurate census that also has privacy protections.

The way I came to know about Cynthia's work was that there was this concern before the 2020 census — and this is all in danah's book — from the nonprofit world. We really want accurate race and ethnicity data, because the Voting Rights Act — rest in peace after _[Callais](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-109_21o3.pdf)_ — used to protect the right to vote for people of color. And you need to know where people of color are in order to protect them.

So all the nonprofit groups would help to do what they called "get out the count." Let's just make sure people get counted. But if you do something like say we're going to have a citizenship question, that freaks everybody out. Not only do non-citizens not want to fill out a census, but citizens don't want to fill out a census either, and then we get garbage data.

And the other side of it is, before the citizenship question came out, a nonprofit could go to communities and say, "Even if you are undocumented, you can fill out this census, and it's important that you do, because there is this protection of privacy." When I learned about the mathematical guarantee, I was like, "That's pretty solid protection." I can tell you this smart person invented this thing to protect your privacy. And so eventually all the civil rights groups helped to get out the count, to make sure that we had as accurate an understanding as we could of the race and ethnicity of people in the country.

And that led to not just, can we draw congressional districts that will elect a candidate of choice of Black voters? That's important. But it matters for the state senate, and for the state legislature. It matters for school boards, public utility districts, port authorities. We elect so many things in America, and there are so many ways to have distortions in representation. I would prefer to be having a fight about whether this is the form of representation we want or that is the form of representation we want, rather than saying all the data is garbage and it doesn't even matter what form of representation we want, because we couldn't even assess whether we have it.

So that's my interest in the census, and seeing the Federal Register notice was deeply distressing. One of the things people have said in the states after _Louisiana v. Callais_, which is the decision that essentially killed the Voting Rights Act, is: are there things we can do to help protect ourselves? I started saying, put into state legislation, just run your own census — this may be crazy, but people used to run their own census — if you want to have an accurate ability to protect. It won't work for Congress, because you need to have accuracy between states, but for the sub-state level, having your own census is the only thing I can think of to be more helpful. So that's where I'm at.

**Jayshree Sarathy:** I'm very excited to be in conversation with these amazing scholars who have worked on this topic longer than I have. I came first to differential privacy in my graduate work, about a decade after Cynthia and others had coined the term. I was really thinking, in 2018, 2019, about improving the statistical and algorithmic analyses that we can do with differential privacy, and at the time with an eye to how it might fit the census use cases or other use cases for federal or official statistics.

A lot of that work was in conversation and collaboration with census scientists, and I was really struck by how committed the scientists from the Census Bureau were, how knowledgeable they were, how much innovation was coming out of government. And this is beautifully documented in danah's book. I was struck by how much work and thought was put into how we can take this definition of privacy, this commitment to privacy, and make it work with the many legal and bureaucratic and operational constraints that go into something as complex as the census.

In that process, I also had this moment of doing a deep dive into the census world and understanding the very interesting dynamics around how statistics and politics interact, and just how infrastructural the census is to our democracy as well as to science in the US. Since then, my research has shifted. In computer science, we were working on all of these exciting algorithmic techniques for differential privacy. We were excited that the Census Bureau was so invested in deploying this new technology, and it seemed like a straightforward modernization of privacy.

So I think myself and other computer scientists around me were really shocked when we saw how political and messy this became in the public. We were like, "This makes a lot of sense to do, and this is a natural progression of the technical advancements of the census. So what is happening? Why is this so contested?"

My research now really tries to understand that, and to bring it back to computer scientists. How can we rigorously think about the intersection of technology, especially privacy technology, with these very complex social and political contexts? How can we more deeply understand the context, and work on both designing tools and communication, to make sure that what we design as well-intended technologies doesn't run into the many different problems it has run into with the census?

Part of that is really understanding, especially with the census, that it was such an eye-opening experience to work in the space, because there are so many different experts and so much knowledge, from the data users, data subjects, advocacy groups, and scientists, all working together on this project to create better data, all with that same goal. And still, there are a lot of challenges with that. So that's how I came to the census, and my fascination is really with how we can understand and create more legitimate data.

## The 2030 Census, Privacy, and Trust

**Molly Hardy:** Great. Thank you so much to all of you for setting the table so nicely. Technology and context, that's something we think a lot about here at the [Berkman Klein Center](https://cyber.harvard.edu/), at the [Public Data Project](https://lil.law.harvard.edu/our-work/public-data-project/), at the [Library Innovation Lab](https://lil.law.harvard.edu/), and I think that's a really good framing here.

So danah, I'm going to start with you, if I may, and ask you about the relationship between democracy and data. Do you think this moment reveals something about the limitations of public data to protect democracy, or is it better thought of as the limitations of democracy to protect public data?

**danah boyd:** Right. One of the things that's so fascinating about census history is that when the idea of creating this country and anchoring a democracy to data started — you know that the moment the data made politics, politics were going to make data. That is the entire history here. Ruth gave you a little taste of the Voting Rights Act, but it's all the way down into all of these different pieces. What has been radically different in recent years has to do with where the site of contestation is.

Let me explain, because there was a previous, really interesting fight over pieces or mechanisms of the census, known as the 1920 case. In 1920, the census came back with reasonable enough data, though it was really hard to collect data during that period because there was a pandemic. At the time, they were having all these technocratic fights. Up until then, the way that Congress did math was, "I don't want any of my friends to lose their seats in the House. We'll just add some more seats for the states that grew bigger faster." It was very funny math. And so people were saying, "Hey, what if we do math better? We can't fit all these people into the Capitol Building, so let's stop growing the size of the House." All of this was coming into being at that 1920 moment.

Then a Harvard economist penned an op-ed on the night before Congress was supposed to meet and decide the reapportionment of the House. Some Southern Democrats were like, "Sweet, we like this economist." His argument was basically that Congress was doing the wrong kind of math. So the Southern Democrats said, "We can't possibly reapportion the House until we figure out the right kind of math." They didn't figure out the right kind of math, and in 1929 the Senate got so annoyed with the House that they pushed through a law that we think of as the Permanent Reapportionment Act.

There are many weird things about that law, but one that is very relevant to the present is that they said, "Well, Congress is so dysfunctional. Despite the fact that the Constitution gave Congress this responsibility, we give up, and we will give the responsibility to the president to figure out apportionment, and that will go great." That, of course, got us into some very interesting fights for 2020.

> Every component piece of the 2030 census will be finalized and going to printing by January of 2029. So I want you to understand that the next two years are the most critical years in this process. And the question is, is Congress going to take responsibility?

So I just want to give you a moment of what's happening right now. That was the story of 2020, but we are living in the story of 2030. For those who don't know census structures, it takes 12 years to plan and produce a census. To give you a sense of the timeline, every component piece of the 2030 census will be finalized and going to printing by January of 2029. So I want you to understand that the next two years are the most critical years in this process. And the question is, is Congress going to take responsibility?

There are three things that happened this summer that I want to flag, and I think we're probably going to drill into more detail on them. The first, which is relevant to these folks, is that every form of noise injection into the census data has been banned. How they define it is really wonky, and we should talk about _[Chevron](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/467/837)_ and _[Loper Bright](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/22-451)_ and a bunch of other things, but one takeaway is: no more differential privacy. The result is that the Census Bureau is now not publishing critical data pieces that we all care about, because they don't know how to resolve these competing expectations.

The second move was the strangest report you could possibly imagine. Basically, a group of civil society actors working on behalf of pro-Trump Republican allies came into the Bureau and managed to weasel their way into this process, and used a certain set of data to produce a [report](https://www.census.gov/library/publications/2026/dir/noncitizen-voting-in-the-2020-election.html) that was showing all of the non-citizens that voted in the 2020 election. Some of you might know that President Trump is convinced that he won the election if it were not for these non-citizens.

Weirdness number one: the report doesn't prove his claim. Even if these were non-citizens, it doesn't prove it. Weirdness number two: this is an unsigned report. No civil servants were involved in the making of it, and they didn't say who was there. No methodology was given, no explanation of error. And everybody's like, "What the heck is this?" Both Republicans and Democrats, economists and demographers, have said, "This is insane." And this is the huge challenge of what it means to think about legitimacy.

The final thing, which is what Ruth has been hitting at, is something called the residence rules. Every decade, the Census Bureau publishes the alterations to the previous residence rules that it wants to make in order to make the next data better. The census is responsible for counting everybody once, only once, and in the right location. The residence rules are the details of how to count people in the right location. In the last couple of decades, the Census Bureau would make tiny micro changes, put them out publicly for comment, and give you thousands of pages of research and documentation telling you why to make these micro changes, which, let's be honest, nobody ever read. Like the worst academic papers ever. But it was — "See? We did the work. This is why it's a good idea."

On September 10, something else happened. [New residence rules came down](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/09/10/2026-18481/decennial-census-of-the-population-of-americans-proposed-residence-criteria-and-proposed-regulations), and they are radical ruptures of everything, complete with a heading that is very political, zero research or explanation of why any of this matters, and all civil servants were told that they are not allowed to speak to the public. And you have 30 days to comment. Comments are due by October 13.

I'll highlight the top three, and I'll do it quickly because I suspect Ruth will have better thoughts on it. One is changing the rules of how we count non-citizens. This is going to be a constitutional fight, because the Constitution is clear that you count all the people, but you can make a rule that says you do it differently, and then lawyers will figure it out.

Number two is that we have a lot of laws in this country, especially from the 1960s and 1970s, that are anchored to race data. We did this because we thought we could invert the collection of race data to address discrimination. So instead of changing the laws, they'll just undermine the existence of the data. That's just great. That's the second point that Ruth is starting to talk about.

The third one is even weirder, and since we're in a geeky audience of nerdy mindsets, I'm just going to lay out one piece of it.
